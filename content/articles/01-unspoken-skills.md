# From Student to Professional: The "Unspoken" Skills That Turn CS Students Into Hirable Engineers

**By Rany Boy Templado** · *5 min read*

`Category: Career Growth / Advice` · `5 min read`

> **Key Takeaway:** Your degree proves you can learn. Your ability to communicate clearly, ask structured questions, and act on gaps is what proves you can be trusted with real systems.

---

School measures you on correct answers. Industry measures you on something quieter: can other people rely on you without babysitting you? I learned this the slow way—moving from support and data operations into SAP data migration, where a single misread requirement can push bad records into a production ledger. The code was rarely the hard part. The skills nobody graded me on were.

Here are the four that moved the needle most.

## 1. Write so people don't have to ask

In a team, your writing *is* your interface. A vague README, a one-line "fixed it" ticket update, a pull request with no description—each one forces a teammate to stop their work and come find you. Multiply that across a sprint and you've become a bottleneck without touching a keyboard.

What clear written communication looks like in practice:

- **READMEs** that answer "how do I run this and what breaks it," not just "what it is."
- **Ticket updates** that state what you did, what's left, and what you're blocked on—so a lead can plan without a meeting.
- **Pull requests** that explain *why* the change exists, not just *what* changed. The diff already shows the what.

You don't need to be a good writer. You need to be a clear one. Short sentences. Concrete nouns. No mystery.

## 2. The 30-minute rule for asking questions

Juniors get stuck on a dial between two failure modes: asking too soon (and looking helpless) or asking too late (and burning a day on something a senior could unblock in two minutes). Here's the rule I give anyone I mentor:

**Struggle productively for 30 minutes. Then ask—but ask a structured question.**

A structured question has four parts:

1. **What I'm trying to do.**
2. **What I tried.**
3. **What happened (the actual error or behavior).**
4. **My best guess at the cause.**

Compare "it's not working, can you help?" with "I'm trying to map legacy customer records into the target structure; I tried the standard field mapping, but the load rejects rows with blank postal codes, and I think the target field is non-nullable—is that right?" The second one respects everyone's time and, half the time, answers itself as you write it.

## 3. Treat ambiguous requirements as the job, not an obstacle

In school, the rubric tells you exactly what "done" means. In industry, requirements arrive half-formed, contradictory, or assumed-obvious by someone who's lived with the system for years. The instinct is to freeze or guess. Both are wrong.

The move is to **make your assumptions visible before you build on them.** Write down how you interpreted the request, list the decisions you had to make, and send it back as a short "here's how I'm reading this—correct me if I'm off." You turn a guess into a checkpoint. Seniors love this because it's cheap to fix a misunderstanding in a sentence and expensive to fix it in code.

Ambiguity isn't the system failing you. Handling it *is* the engineering.

## 4. Own the gaps nobody assigned

Every real project has holes: a flaky setup step, missing docs, a manual process everyone grumbles about but no one owns. In school these aren't your problem. In industry, the engineers who get trusted with bigger things are the ones who quietly close those gaps—write the missing doc, script the annoying manual step, flag the risk before it becomes an incident.

This isn't about doing extra for its own sake. It's about demonstrating that you see the system, not just your ticket. That's the shift from "student who completes tasks" to "engineer who improves things."

---

## The through-line

None of these are technical skills. All of them are engineering skills. They share one root: **reducing the load you put on the people around you.** Clear writing, good questions, visible assumptions, owned gaps—each one makes you someone a team can build on.

Get the fundamentals of your stack, yes. But the students who become hirable engineers are the ones who figure out, early, that software is a team sport played mostly in words.
