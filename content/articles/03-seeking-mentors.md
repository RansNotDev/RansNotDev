# Seeking Mentors Early: How Feedback Loops Shorten Your Learning Curve in Engineering

**By Rany Boy Templado** · *4 min read*

`Category: Mentorship / Personal Development` · `4 min read`

> **Key Takeaway:** You can learn alone, but you'll learn slowly and crookedly. A mentor's feedback loop corrects your direction before you've walked a mile the wrong way.

---

Learning to build software without feedback is like practicing a sport with your eyes closed. You'll get stronger, but you'll also quietly reinforce bad form—and you won't know until it costs you. A mentor isn't someone who hands you answers. A mentor is someone who **shortens the distance between a mistake and noticing it.**

That distance—the feedback loop—is the single biggest lever on how fast you actually grow.

## Why the loop matters more than the lesson

Any concept you need is online. Information isn't the bottleneck. **Correction** is. Left alone, you might spend three weeks writing code a certain way, convinced it's fine, until it breaks in production or a reviewer winces. A mentor compresses those three weeks into a two-minute "don't do it that way, here's why."

Multiply that compression across hundreds of small decisions and you see the real effect: two engineers with identical raw talent diverge enormously based purely on how tight their feedback loops are. The one getting corrected early is, a year later, operating at a different level—not because they were smarter, but because they wasted less time being wrong.

## From "passes the tests" to "clean and maintainable"

School rewards code that produces the right output. Industry needs code the *next person* can read, change, and trust six months from now. That gap is invisible from the inside—your code looks fine to you because you wrote it.

This is where mentors earn their value. A senior reviewing your work sees the things you can't:

- The clever shortcut that'll confuse everyone who touches it later.
- The missing error handling for the input you assumed would never happen.
- The structure that works now but won't survive the next requirement.

"It passes" and "it's good" are different claims. Only someone with more scars than you can reliably tell you which one you've actually achieved. That feedback is how your work matures from *functional* to *professional*.

## Mentors give you direction, not just correction

Beyond code, mentors answer the questions you don't even know to ask. Which skills are worth your limited time? Is this role a dead end or a launchpad? Is the frustration you're feeling normal, or a signal to change something?

Someone who has walked the path you're on can see the map you're still drawing. That perspective—"you're closer than you think," or "don't over-invest here"—saves you from months of pointed-in-the-wrong-direction effort. Correction improves your current step. Direction improves which path you're on.

## Where to find them (it's less formal than you think)

Students imagine mentorship as a formal arrangement. It almost never is. Mentors are:

- **Senior engineers** who answer your well-formed questions in a community or at work.
- **Community leads** running the meetup or Discord you already attend.
- **Upperclassmen** one or two years ahead—close enough to remember your exact struggles.

And the way to attract them is the same every time: **be worth mentoring.** Do the 30 minutes of trying first. Ask structured questions. Act on the advice you're given and report back. Nobody invests in someone who doesn't use the investment. The students who get mentored are the ones who make mentoring them feel rewarding.

## Start the loop now

- Pick **one person** slightly ahead of you and ask a single sharp, specific question.
- When you get code feedback, **apply it and show the result.** That one habit turns a one-off answer into an ongoing relationship.
- Don't wait to feel "ready." The earlier the loop starts, the less wrong-direction walking you'll have to undo.

---

## The through-line

Raw effort gets you moving. Feedback keeps you moving in the right direction. The engineers who grow fastest aren't the ones who grind hardest in isolation—they're the ones who built tight feedback loops early and let other people's experience correct their course.

Find your loop. Start it before you think you're ready.
