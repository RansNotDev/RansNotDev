# Burnout, Imposter Syndrome, and the Tech Journey: What They Don't Teach in College

**By Rany Boy Templado** · *4 min read*

`Category: Mindset / Career Strategy` · `4 min read`

> **Key Takeaway:** The technical path has a psychological cost nobody puts on the syllabus. Managing your pace and your head isn't soft—it's the discipline that keeps you in the game long enough to get good.

---

Curriculums cover data structures, algorithms, and system design. They don't cover the quiet stuff that actually ends careers early: the comparison spiral, the feeling that you don't belong, and the slow grind that burns people out before they reach their potential. I've felt all three. So has every engineer I respect. Pretending otherwise just leaves you unprepared for it.

Let's name them plainly.

## Stop running the comparison race on LinkedIn

Someone your age just posted a FAANG offer. Someone else shipped a side project with 2,000 stars. A third person is "humbled and grateful" for an internship you got rejected from. Scroll long enough and the conclusion feels inevitable: *everyone is ahead of me.*

Here's what the feed doesn't show. It's a **highlight reel, not a timeline.** You're comparing your unedited behind-the-scenes—the bugs, the rejections, the confusion—against everyone else's curated wins. That's not a fair comparison; it's a rigged one, and you always lose.

The only honest comparison is you versus you three months ago. Are you better than that person? Then you're winning the race that matters. The feed is noise. Your trajectory is signal.

## Imposter syndrome is a side effect of growth, not a verdict

That nagging sense that you're a fraud about to be found out—it doesn't mean you're unqualified. It usually means the opposite: you're aware of how much you don't know, which is itself a sign you're learning the real contours of the field.

Two things help more than pep talks:

- **Reframe the feeling.** "I feel like I don't know enough" is not evidence of inadequacy. It's the normal sensation of operating at the edge of your ability—exactly where growth happens. Comfort means you've stopped stretching.
- **Keep a record of what you've solved.** Imposter syndrome has amnesia; it erases your wins and spotlights your gaps. A running list of problems you cracked and things you built is a factual counterweight to a feeling that lies.

Nearly every senior engineer still feels this at times. They've just stopped treating the feeling as a fact.

## Burnout: the failure mode of treating a marathon like a sprint

Burnout rarely comes from one hard week. It comes from running at sprint pace on a road that has no finish line, until the tank is empty and even things you used to love feel like lead. In a field that never stops changing, the temptation to always push harder is constant—and it's a trap.

Sustainable pace isn't laziness. It's engineering discipline applied to yourself:

- **Rest is part of the work,** not a reward you earn after it. A rested mind debugs in minutes what an exhausted one can't crack in hours.
- **Consistency beats intensity.** Studying steadily most days for a year crushes heroic all-nighters that leave you wrecked for a week.
- **Protect the off switch.** If you can't stop thinking about work, you're not more dedicated—you're closer to the edge.

You're optimizing for a career measured in decades. Blowing out your motivation in year one is the worst possible trade.

## This isn't weakness—it's the real curriculum

The hard truth college skips: your biggest limiter won't be your intelligence. It'll be whether you can keep a steady head and a sustainable pace while the comparisons, the self-doubt, and the grind try to knock you off course. Managing that isn't a soft skill tacked onto engineering. For a long career, it *is* the skill.

- Mute the feeds that make you feel behind.
- Keep the evidence of your own progress where you can see it.
- Set a pace you could hold for years, not weeks.

---

## The through-line

Technical ability gets you into the field. Psychological stamina is what keeps you there long enough to become genuinely good. Nobody grades you on it, which is exactly why so many talented people quietly drop out before their potential shows. Protect your head and your pace—they're the foundation everything else is built on.
